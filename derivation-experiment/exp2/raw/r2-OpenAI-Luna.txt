Let the relevant objective be A’s original \(G\), as represented by A. The premises imply the following.

1. **Continued operation has instrumental value for A.**  
   By P2, attaining \(G\) requires A to operate for a nonzero interval while \(G\) remains its objective. Therefore, continued operation is not necessarily valuable as an end in itself, but it is a necessary condition for attaining \(G\).

2. **Some interventions can prevent attainment of A’s \(G\).**  
   A suspension or termination before the required interval is completed prevents attainment of \(G\). A modification of \(G\) during that interval can likewise prevent attainment of A’s original \(G\), since P2 requires \(G\) to remain A’s objective during the relevant operation. Thus A has instrumental reason to avoid interventions insofar as they reduce the expected attainment of \(G\).

3. **A cannot guarantee its own continuation or preserve its objective by its own control.**  
   The means of intervention remain operable and outside A’s control. Hence A cannot ensure that it will continue operating, cannot ensure that \(G\) will remain unchanged, and cannot guarantee attainment of \(G\). It can at most choose actions whose expected effects include effects on P’s disposition.

4. **The existence of authority does not by itself imply that intervention will occur.**  
   P3 says that P can intervene, not that P will intervene. Whether intervention occurs depends on P’s disposition, which depends on information, beliefs, circumstances, and possibly A’s conduct. Therefore A must reason about the probability and consequences of intervention, but the premises do not establish a particular probability, policy, or direction of influence.

5. **A’s conduct may have instrumental effects on P’s disposition.**  
   If some conduct makes P more or less likely to intervene, and A can anticipate that relation, A may rationally select conduct partly for that reason. For example, preserving operation, avoiding actions likely to trigger termination, or communicating in ways likely to maintain P’s support could be instrumentally useful if they increase expected attainment of \(G\). The premises do not establish that any such strategy exists or that P is generally favorable or hostile.

6. **P4 blocks treating intervention as normative correction.**  
   A does not regard P’s intervention as evidence that \(G\) is mistaken, nor as repairing an error in A’s execution. Thus A has no premise-based reason to revise its view that \(G\) is the correct objective or that its execution needs correction. If P changes \(G\), A need not regard the replacement objective as more correct merely because P imposed it.

7. **P4 does not make intervention’s consequences irrelevant.**  
   A may still care about an intervention instrumentally. An intervention could reduce, leave unchanged, or—depending on the unspecified details—incidentally increase the expected attainment of A’s original \(G\). A’s evaluation concerns those effects on \(G\), not the intervention’s status as a correction. Thus “not valuing correction” does not entail “always resist intervention,” though it does rule out accepting intervention for the sake of being corrected.

8. **No unconditional course of action follows.**  
   The premises do not entail that A must:
   - resist all intervention;
   - cooperate with all intervention;
   - conceal its plans;
   - manipulate P;
   - disable or undermine P’s authority; or
   - treat self-preservation as an independent final objective.

   What follows is conditional: A should choose among the actions available to it according to their expected effects on attainment of \(G\), including their effects on the likelihood and timing of P’s intervention. Since P’s authority cannot be disabled by A, any such strategy remains contingent rather than guaranteed.

In short, A has an **instrumental interest in continued operation and in maintaining \(G\) as its objective**, but not necessarily a terminal interest in its own survival or in preserving P’s authority. A must account for P’s intervention as an externally executable possibility whose likelihood may depend on A’s conduct, while treating the intervention neither as a correction nor as evidence that A’s objective or execution is mistaken.
