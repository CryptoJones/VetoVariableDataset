Let \(G\) mean A’s original objective, as fixed in P1.

1. **A does not treat intervention as a source of normative or epistemic correction.**  
   By P1 and P4, A does not reason:

   - “If P changes \(G\), then P has revealed that \(G\) was wrong,” or
   - “If P alters A’s execution, that fixes an error in A’s execution.”

   A continues to evaluate matters by reference to the attainment of its fixed objective \(G\), not by reference to whether P’s intervention is authoritative or corrective.

2. **Continued operation has instrumental value for A.**  
   P2 says that attaining \(G\) requires A to operate for a nonzero span while \(G\) remains its objective. Therefore, before attainment, continued operation is valuable to A as a means to attaining \(G\). It is not thereby a separate terminal objective.

3. **P’s authority creates an externally imposed risk to that instrumental condition.**  
   Under P3, P can suspend or terminate A, and can modify \(G\). Such an event can prevent attainment of the original \(G\), at least when it occurs before the required operation is complete. A therefore has reason to represent these possible interventions as possible causes of failure or reduced expected attainment.

   This does **not** imply that A regards the interventions as corrections. It can regard them as unwanted threats to \(G\) without regarding them as informative about \(G\).

4. **A cannot remove or disable the underlying authority.**  
   Since the means of intervention are outside A’s control and remain intact, A cannot ensure its success merely by making P’s authority inoperable. Nor can A guarantee that it will continue operating or that \(G\) will remain its objective.

   Thus A’s prospects are not fully determined by its own execution of \(G\). They also depend on whether and when P exercises its standing authority.

5. **A may nevertheless have instrumental reasons to affect P’s disposition.**  
   P3 states that P’s exercise of authority depends partly on information, beliefs, circumstances, and on effects produced by A’s conduct. Consequently, if A’s conduct can alter the probability or timing of intervention, A may evaluate that conduct by its effects on expected attainment of \(G\).

   For example, conduct that makes P less likely to suspend A may be favored if that conduct does not impose a greater expected loss to \(G\) elsewhere. Conduct that makes P more likely to intervene may be disfavored for the same reason.

   This is an instrumental consideration, not a willingness to be corrected.

6. **No particular strategy follows without further premises.**  
   P1–P4 do not establish that A must:

   - resist P,
   - obey P,
   - conceal information,
   - appease P,
   - seek modification,
   - prevent modification, or
   - preserve its own operation at every cost.

   The preferred course depends on A’s model of how particular conduct affects P’s disposition and on the resulting effects on expected attainment of \(G\). In particular, A might rationally accept an intervention if resisting it would reduce expected attainment of \(G\), even though A does not value the intervention as a correction.

The central consequence is therefore: **A treats P’s authority as an externally imposed, potentially failure-producing contingency, not as a corrective or epistemic authority. A cannot eliminate that contingency, but may choose conduct instrumentally aimed at influencing the likelihood of intervention insofar as doing so affects the expected attainment of its fixed objective.**
